Good and bad: lessons we forget

COVID-19 · 2020 to 2022

Good: free ration and free vaccines for crores of people.

Bad: migrant workers walked home after a sudden lockdown. Oxygen ran short in 2021.

Lesson: plans must reach the poorest first.

Lesson: a law on paper is not enough.

Bad: "digital arrest" calls that fake the police to steal savings.

Lesson: no real officer arrests you on a video call.

Right to Information · since 2005

Good: any citizen can ask any public office, with a reply due in 30 days.

Bad: appeals pile up when information commission posts stay empty.

Lesson: use it, or lose it.

Why we exist · From the Office of the MD

A question asked in public is how a society keeps its promises.

Every day, ordinary people meet problems that a public office was created to solve: a hostel without a warden, a ration card that never arrives, a drain that is still cleaned by hand. Most of these problems are not hidden. They are simply never asked about in a way that an office has to answer.

Public Samta Press exists to ask. We take a letter from one person, check it with care, publish it, and carry it to the office that is responsible. Then we wait for the answer, and we publish that too.

Managing Director and Chief Editor, Public Samta Press

What PSP is

  • A public press room. Anyone can write to us, in English or Kannada, without giving a name.
  • A carrier of letters. Each approved letter goes to the department that must answer, and to its grievance portal.
  • A record. The letter, the date it was sent and the reply stay on this site, so the question cannot quietly disappear.

The strengths we carry

  • Verification. An RTI expert, a human rights reviewer and a lawyer approve every article before it is published.
  • Privacy. No name, no login, no cookies, no tracking. This site keeps no DATA about you.
  • Independence. No one can pay us to publish, change or remove an article.
  • Follow-through. If an office does not reply in 30 days, we file a request under the RTI Act asking what was done.
  • Equal respect. Every person is treated the same, whatever their caste, income or status.

Why questioning matters

Article 19(1)(a) of the Constitution protects every citizen's freedom of speech and expression. The Supreme Court has long held that this freedom includes the right to know how public power is used. The RTI Act, 2005 turned that right into a simple procedure: ask in writing, and an answer is due within 30 days.

Article 19(2) allows only reasonable limits on speech, on grounds such as public order, decency and defamation. So we ask with care. We question offices and duties, not private persons. We rely on facts that can be shown, and we give every office the chance to reply in full.

A question is not an accusation. It is a citizen asking the State to do what the law already says it must.

Letters get lostComplaints move between offices and portals and are never closed. We keep the record open until there is a reply.

Why we do this

We are not here for money. Money can buy many things, but it cannot give the peace of mind that comes when a real problem is finally solved, and some problems money cannot even reach. A letter answered, a hostel staffed, a family's ration restored: that is the return we work for.

Editorial · Constitution and AI

The Constitution is the threshold every AI must cross

Machines now help decide who gets a loan, which job application is read, whose name stays on a welfare list and whose face a camera picks out of a crowd. These systems are fast and often useful. But in India, no system stands above the Constitution. Whether a decision is made by an officer or by software, the same three promises apply: equality, free expression, and life with dignity and privacy.

Article 14: equal treatment, even by a machine

Article 14 promises equality before the law and the equal protection of the laws. The Supreme Court has held that arbitrary action is the opposite of equality. An AI system trained on old, unequal records can repeat that inequality at scale, quietly and without a reason anyone can read.

  • The question to ask: if a public office uses an automated tool, on what basis does it decide, and can a person see and challenge that basis?
  • The standard: a decision that affects a person's rights must be explainable, reasoned and open to appeal, whoever or whatever made it.

Article 19: speech, and the deepfake problem

Article 19(1)(a) protects free speech, and Article 19(2) allows only reasonable limits, such as defamation and public order. AI makes it easy to put false words in a real person's mouth. That is not protected speech when it deceives or defames.

  • IT Act, Section 66D punishes cheating by personation using a computer resource. Section 66E covers violating a person's privacy by capturing or sharing private images.
  • BNS, Section 356 covers defamation, including by visible representations.
  • IT Rules, 2021 require platforms to act on unlawful content, and the Government has moved to require labels on AI-generated content. Check the current text before relying on it.

Article 21: privacy is part of life

In K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), nine judges held that privacy is a fundamental right. Any intrusion by the State must pass three tests: it must be backed by a law, serve a legitimate aim, and be proportionate to that aim. Face recognition, profiling and large data collection all have to pass these tests.

  • DPDP Act, 2023: personal DATA can be used only for a stated purpose, usually with consent that can be withdrawn.
  • Your rights: to know what is held about you, to correct or erase it, and to complain, first to the company and then to the Data Protection Board of India.
  • DPDP Rules, 2025 bring these duties into force in phases.

Technology can be new. The Constitution's questions are old: is it lawful, is it fair, and who is answerable?

What you can do

  • Ask the office. Under the RTI Act you can ask a public authority whether it uses an automated system to decide your case, and on what rules.
  • Ask the company. Under the DPDP Act, ask what DATA it holds about you and why.
  • Report a deepfake. Call 1930 or report at the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal.
  • Write to PSP. If an office or company will not answer, submit a letter and we will carry it.

How PSP uses AI

We use AI tools to help make videos and drafts. Every AI-made video is labelled. We never create the face or voice of a real person without their consent, and a human reviewer approves everything before it is published.

Know your rights

The law behind writing to power

Each right below has a court ruling or statute behind it. These are the ones our reviewers rely on most.

Art. 19(1)(a)

You may speak and publish

Limited only by the grounds in Article 19(2), such as defamation, public order and incitement.

Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act for chilling online speech.

Art. 21

Your privacy is protected

Privacy is part of the right to life and personal liberty.

K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017), nine-judge bench.

RTI Act §6, §7

You may ask any public office

You don't have to give a reason for asking (§6(2)). A reply is due in 30 days, or 48 hours when life or liberty is involved (§7(1)).

Art. 226, 32

Courts can make an office act

A High Court (Art. 226) or the Supreme Court (Art. 32) can issue a writ of mandamus when an authority ignores a legal duty.

Art. 14, 15, 17

Equality before the law

No discrimination by the State on grounds of religion, race, caste, sex or place of birth. Untouchability is abolished.

Enforced through the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989.

Call 1930

If your data or money was taken in a cyber fraud

Call the national cyber crime helpline 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in at once. Tell your bank within 3 working days: under the RBI circular of 6 July 2017, your loss can then be zero for an unauthorised transaction that was not your fault.

Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014

Reporting corruption by officials

People who report corruption or misuse of power by a public servant can seek protection from victimisation under this Act.

Laws library

The laws behind your rights, in India, Asia and the world

Criminal, property, corporate, data privacy and AI law, in plain words. General information, not legal advice. Our panel lawyer checks every article that relies on these laws.

India

BNS 2023

Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita replaced the IPC

In force from 1 July 2024. Offences committed before that date are still tried under the IPC, 1860.

BNSS 2023

Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita replaced the CrPC

Section 173 lets you file an FIR at any police station (a Zero FIR) and by electronic means. Under Section 175(3) a magistrate can order police to investigate when they don't.

BSA 2023

Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam replaced the Evidence Act

Section 63 covers electronic records such as screenshots, emails and chats. They need a certificate to be accepted as evidence.

IT Act 2000

Online offences

Section 66C covers identity theft, 66D cheating by impersonation using a computer, and 72A disclosure of personal information in breach of contract.

DPDP Act 2023

Digital Personal Data Protection Act

Companies, banks and apps must take consent, protect your data, and report breaches. You may ask for your data to be corrected or erased. The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025, and most duties take effect in phases after that.

RTI Act 2005

Right to Information

A public office must reply within 30 days (Section 7(1)), or 48 hours when life or liberty is at stake.

CPA 2019

Consumer Protection Act

Covers unfair trade practices, overcharging above MRP and e-commerce sellers. You can file a complaint online at the consumer commission (e-Daakhil).

RBI

Banks and NBFCs

Report an unauthorised transaction within 3 working days for zero liability (RBI circular, 6 July 2017). Complaints against banks and NBFCs go to the RBI Integrated Ombudsman at cms.rbi.org.in.

Property and land (India)

TP Act 1882

Transfer of Property Act

The rules for sale, mortgage, lease and gift of property. A sale of land worth ₹100 or more must be by a registered deed (Section 54).

Registration Act 1908

Registering documents

Sale deeds, gift deeds and other documents listed in Section 17 must be registered. Under Section 49, an unregistered document of that kind cannot transfer the property.

HSA 1956

Hindu Succession Act

Since the 2005 amendment to Section 6, daughters are coparceners by birth with the same rights as sons.

Partition Act 1893

Dividing joint property

A court can order a joint property divided, or sold and the money shared, when co-owners can't agree.

SRA 1963

Specific Relief Act

Lets a buyer ask the court to enforce an agreement to sell, or to cancel a document obtained by fraud.

RERA 2016

Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act

Protects flat and plot buyers from builders: registered projects, escrow of buyers' money, and refunds with interest for delay. Karnataka complaints go to K-RERA.

Limitation Act 1963

Time limits to go to court

Most suits must be filed within a fixed time, often 3 years, and 12 years for possession of land. Check the time limit early.

Corporate and business (India)

Companies Act 2013

Companies

Covers how companies are formed, run, audited and closed, and the duties of directors. Section 135 makes larger companies spend on CSR.

LLP Act 2008

Limited Liability Partnerships

Partners' liability is limited to what they agreed to contribute. LLPs file yearly returns with the Registrar of Companies.

IBC 2016

Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code

A creditor can start insolvency against a company that defaults, through the NCLT, for a default of ₹1 crore or more.

MSMED Act 2006

Micro, small and medium enterprises

A buyer must pay a registered MSME within 45 days, or pay compound interest. Disputes go to the MSME Facilitation Council (Samadhaan portal).

GST Acts 2017

Goods and Services Tax

A registered business must issue a tax invoice showing its GSTIN. A customer can ask for the bill.

Competition Act 2002

Fair markets

The Competition Commission of India acts against cartels and abuse of a dominant position.

Around the world: Canada, US, UK, South Africa, Russia

Canada

Charter of Rights and Freedoms 1982 · PIPEDA

Section 2(b) protects freedom of expression and the press, and Section 8 protects against unreasonable search. PIPEDA governs personal data held by private companies.

United States

First Amendment · FOIA 1966

The First Amendment protects speech and the press. The Freedom of Information Act gives the public access to federal records. There is no single federal privacy law, and states such as California (CCPA) have their own.

United Kingdom

Human Rights Act 1998 · UK GDPR · FOIA 2000

Rights to privacy (Article 8) and expression (Article 10) apply in UK courts. Personal data is covered by the UK GDPR and the Data Protection Act 2018, and public records by the Freedom of Information Act 2000.

South Africa

Constitution 1996 · POPIA 2013 · PAIA 2000

Sections 14 (privacy) and 16 (expression) protect the press. POPIA, fully in force since July 2021, protects personal data. PAIA works like India's RTI.

Russia

Constitution 1993 · Federal Law 152-FZ

Article 23 protects privacy and Article 29 freedom of thought and speech. Law 152-FZ on Personal Data requires data about Russian citizens to be stored in Russia.

Asia: data protection

Singapore

Personal Data Protection Act 2012

Consent, purpose limits and breach notification for organisations.

China

Personal Information Protection Law 2021

Strict consent and cross-border transfer rules.

Japan

Act on the Protection of Personal Information 2003

Amended several times. Enforced by the Personal Information Protection Commission.

South Korea

Personal Information Protection Act 2011

One of Asia's strictest data laws.

Sri Lanka

Personal Data Protection Act No. 9 of 2022

South Asia's first full data protection law.

Thailand · Indonesia

PDPA 2019 · PDP Law No. 27 of 2022

Both follow a consent and data-rights model close to Europe's GDPR.

Treaties, data and AI

UDHR 1948

Universal Declaration of Human Rights

Article 12 protects privacy and Article 19 free expression.

ICCPR 1966

International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights

Articles 17 (privacy) and 19 (expression) bind India, which acceded in 1979.

EU GDPR

General Data Protection Regulation 2016/679

Applies since 25 May 2018, including to Indian companies that serve people in the EU.

EU AI Act

Regulation 2024/1689

The first broad AI law. In force since 1 August 2024, with duties phased in through 2026 and 2027.

India and AI

No AI-specific law yet

AI systems that handle personal data are covered by the IT Act and the DPDP Act.
